What caused the AI winters

First AI winter (1974 – 1980)

Causes: The 1973 Lighthill report judged AI to have failed its big promises, DARPA cut general academic AI funding in 1973 and 1974, and machine translation and perceptron limits had already dented confidence.

Cost: UK AI research was largely dismantled outside a few universities such as Edinburgh, Essex and Sussex, and DARPA redirected money to projects with specific, measurable goals. The pattern: pessimism among researchers, then in the press, then a funding cut.

Second AI winter (1987 – 1993)

Causes: The LISP machine market collapsed in 1987, and hand-written rule bases proved brittle, with maintenance costs growing beyond the value they returned.

Cost: Companies abandoned expert systems and funding and enthusiasm fell again, though some work continued underneath, including neural networks applied to real tasks. Dating varies by source.

Why this matters to you

The two winters show what happens when hype outruns measurable results, and the revival shows that progress returns once data, hardware and methods catch up. As an engineer with 5+ years of production backend and cloud experience in financial services and graduate-level AI training, I focus on building AI-enabled systems that are reliable, secure, explainable and tied to measurable goals.

Reflection

Customizing for my audience. I wrote for hiring managers, recruiters and engineering leads, so I kept entries short, added era filters so they can find what matters, and tied the history to my own engineering and AI background.

Lessons learned. The two AI winters were the biggest lesson: progress stalled when promises outran results. That shapes how I plan and communicate AI projects.

How I applied it. I changed the home page so the typing stops after three rounds on a steady message, and added a scrolling services ticker that can be paused.

1. Deep learning and neural networks

Artificial neural networks learn by adjusting weights to reduce error. Convolutional networks (CNNs) find patterns in images, recurrent networks (RNNs) handle sequences such as text, and generative adversarial networks (GANs) pit two networks against each other to create new data. Activation functions let a network learn curved, not just straight, patterns. Synthetic data such as deepfakes raises real ethical questions about trust and consent.

ANNCNNRNNGANActivation functions
🎓

2. How language models are trained

In a guided exercise with an AI tutor ("Professor Predict"), I explored how large language models learn. Pretraining teaches a model to predict the next token. Post-training makes it follow instructions. Neither is a guaranteed fact-checker, so fluent answers can still be wrong (hallucination). Search, calculators, supplied documents and human review cover the gaps. My rule: use a model for drafting, explaining and brainstorming, and verify anything live, exact, private or high-stakes.

Change sticks when people understand it. I practiced clear, consistent messages, two-way feedback, storytelling and chunking complex ideas into small pieces with visuals and hands-on practice. I applied these to the Einstein challenge: explain neural networks simply (below).

Three leaders gave me a pattern for hard seasons. Moses accepted advice and shared the load by delegating. Nehemiah prayed first, then planned, and adjusted when opposition came. Esther prepared carefully, picked her moment and took a risk for others. My takeaway: adapt with humility, ask for help early, and make decisions for the good of the people I serve.

Interactive · Neural networks explained simply

Neural Network Playground

The Einstein challenge: if you cannot explain it simply, you do not understand it well enough. My simple explanation: a neural network is a team of very simple decision-makers in layers. Each weighs its inputs, decides how loudly to speak up, and passes the result on. At first the team guesses badly. After each guess we tell it how wrong it was, and every member nudges its weights a little. After thousands of nudges, the team gets good. That is learning: practice with feedback.
Try it: train a tiny network to separate orange dots from blue ones. Remove the activation function and watch it fail on the XOR pattern, because without a bend it can only draw one straight line. Add neurons and watch it succeed.

Reflection

Customizing for my audience. I wrote for hiring managers and technical leads who value clear explanations, so I used plain words and an analogy first, then let them try a small network themselves. The page has clear instructions, a Pause button, and the lab is optional so it never gets in the way.

Lessons learned. Neural networks learn through feedback and many small adjustments, and activation functions are what let them learn curved patterns. Language models produce plausible text, not guaranteed facts, so they need checking. I also learned that Moses, Nehemiah and Esther lead through humility, preparation and courage, and that leading a team improves the same way a network does.

How I applied it. I kept this demo simple, put the directions up front, added messages that explain what is going wrong (for example, why a network with no activation function fails), and kept the code small and documented.
